6Wear & tie barsHow worn the tread is, and which bars bridge its grooves.
How a tie bar is treated

Put the groove bottom at zero. A block's top starts at NSD and, after the tyre has worn w mm, sits at NSD − w. A tie bar is a raised strip of that groove bottom of height h < NSD, so its top starts NSD − h below the road and touches nothing.

It engages once w ≥ NSD − h. From then on it is flush with the blocks and is simply more land at the same height — which is the point of a tie bar: it arrives part-worn and stiffens the block row against the heel-and-toe wear that is developing by then.

So at the default wear = 0 the bars are listed and drawn but contribute nothing, which is the truth for a new tyre. Force contact overrides that for what-if work: the bar is analysed as a short proud block of its own height, which is as close as a surface-contact model can get.

No tie bars found in this drawing. A tie bar is picked up as a closed region between two blocks whose long sides are the neighbouring groove walls — it does not need an outline of its own. If your drawing has bars that are not listed, check that the lines closing them across the groove actually meet the walls (raise the weld tolerance in 1 · Tread plan), or that the bar is not larger than the tie-bar area limit.

Slip response: Cα, Cκ and the pneumatic trail

Kx and Ky say how hard the rubber in the patch is. They are not forces. A tyre only makes a force when it slips, so three of the rows above add the step that turns stiffness into force — the brush model. A tread element enters the patch stuck to the road with no deflection; while the carcass runs on, the element is dragged by κ·s longitudinally or tan α·s laterally, where s is how far it has travelled since it entered. Adding up k·deflection over the patch gives

  • Cκ = ∂Fx/∂κ = Σ kx·s — longitudinal slip stiffness, in N per unit slip ratio.
  • Cα = ∂Fy/∂α = Σ ky·s — cornering stiffness, in N/rad. Multiply by π/180 for newtons per degree of slip.
  • Pneumatic trail t — how far behind the patch centre the lateral force actually acts, in mm. Aligning moment Mz = Cα·t·α.

These are first moments about the leading edge, not sums, so they are not Kx and Ky rescaled: rubber near the exit has been dragged furthest and counts for far more than rubber at the entry. That is also why they are direction-sensitive — reverse the rolling direction and the same tread gives a different curve, which no other row on this page can show. Rotation runs in the direction of travel, so a block enters the patch at high θ and leaves at low θ.

Read the variation, not the absolute value. The tread is one spring in series with the carcass and belt, and on a real tyre the carcass usually dominates the cornering stiffness. What you are seeing here is the tread's share, and how much it swings as the pattern rotates — that part is yours to design.

What this chart is

It answers one question: as the wheel turns once, how many times does the tyre stiffness rise and fall, and by how much?

Take any curve from the θ sweep — say Kz — and it wobbles up and down as blocks enter and leave the contact patch. This chart breaks that wobble into its repeating rhythms.

  • Order (the x-axis) is how many times per wheel revolution something repeats. Order 24 means it happens 24 times per turn. At 60 km/h a typical tyre turns about 8 times a second, so order 24 lands near 200 Hz — squarely in the range the ear is most sensitive to.
  • Amplitude (the y-axis) is how big that wobble is, as a percentage of the average. A bar at 4% means the stiffness swings ±4% about its mean, that many times per revolution.
  • The red bar marks your pitch count, and the orange bar the geometric repeat of the drawing. Energy landing there is the pattern's own rhythm.

How to read it

  • One tall bar towering over the rest — the tyre hits the same note over and over. That is what a tonal whine sounds like, and it is the classic reason to modulate a pitch sequence.
  • Energy spread over many orders — the same total wobble smeared across frequencies, which the ear reads as broadband hiss rather than a tone. This is usually what you want.
  • Low orders (1–5) standing out — something varies once or twice per revolution. That is not a noise problem; it points at non-uniformity, a shoulder imbalance, or a pattern that does not close cleanly at the seam.
  • Bars beyond about order 60 are finer than this grid resolves reliably; treat them as indicative.

Comparing designs is where this earns its keep. The absolute heights depend on load, patch size and compound, so a single chart is hard to judge on its own. Two designs on the same settings, though, are directly comparable: the one with its energy spread wider is the quieter pitch sequence.

The tread arc profile, and what camber does with it

The crown is stored as a local radius at every lateral position, and everything about lean follows from integrating it. The tangent angle is φ(y) = ∫ dy/r(y), the drop below the centreline is z(y) = ∫ sin φ dy.

  • Where the tyre touches at lean γ is the point where φ(y) = γ — the tread tangent has rotated to horizontal there. Lean the tyre and the contact point walks outboard along the profile.
  • The patch shape comes from the local radii at that point: R_lat = r(y_c) across the tread and R_eff = wheel radius − z(y_c) along it. A crown that tightens toward the shoulder narrows the patch as you lean.
  • Maximum reachable lean is the steepest tangent the profile reaches, max|φ|. Past it there is no contact point at all, and those lean angles are dropped from the sweep rather than extrapolated.

Type a real arc specification in 5 · Load & advanced — 800@45mm, 300@88mm, 90 — for true arcs meeting tangentially at the breakpoints. Leaving it blank uses the two-radius blend instead, which is smoother than a real profile through the shoulder transition.

Violet outlines are the tie bars; the thin violet lines are the bonded links the network above is actually solving — one per shared wall, joining a bar to the block it is tied to. A bar with no line touches nothing along a shared edge and contributes no coupling stiffness, however solid it looks. A bar still below the tread surface is drawn dotted: it adds no contact area yet, but it is already coupling the blocks either side of it, which is why the gain is there from new.

What this is

Everywhere else in this tool a block is an independent spring. Its stiffness comes from its own outline and nothing else, and the patch total is the sum of the springs it covers. That is what makes the θ sweep an FFT — and it is exactly wrong for a tie bar, whose whole purpose is to make neighbouring blocks not independent.

So this tab solves the real thing. Blocks and tie bars are nodes, each with two degrees of freedom and its own stiffness to the belt. Every bar is bonded to the blocks it touches by a link carrying the rubber between them. The assembly is solved, and the answer covers every case with no special handling: a bar with a free wall on one side, two bars of different heights on the same block, a whole rib tied end to end.

Reading it

  • Uncoupled is the tread as independent springs — what the other tabs show. Coupled is the same tread solved as a network. Both are measured the same way, so the gain between them is the tie bars' contribution and nothing else.
  • Kxy is the cross term: push the tread circumferentially and it also moves laterally. Bars square to the tread give none. A diagonal bar does, and that is a real steering effect you would otherwise not see.
  • The gain is largest when few blocks are loaded and smallest when the patch covers a whole tied rib. That is not an artefact — blocks moving together put no load into the bars at all.

Two mechanisms, at opposite ends of the tyre's life

The coupling here works from new and its relative value falls as the tread wears: a block stiffens as roughly 1/L³ as it shortens, while the bar only shrinks once the tread reaches it. The contact-area contribution is the opposite — nothing until worn into, then land like any block. Tie bars are useful across the whole life because the two mechanisms hand over, not because they add up.

What this model assumes.
  • A bar is a linear spring bonded over (shared wall length × bar height), spanning from that wall to the bar's centre — axial along the span, shear across it.
  • One point per block, two translational degrees of freedom. The bar sits below the block top, so it really applies a couple as well as a force; that moment is not modelled, which makes the bars read slightly soft.
  • Rigid belt, uniform patch pressure, small displacement, linear elastic — the same assumptions as everywhere else in the tool.
  • Absolute values here use a force-controlled definition and will not match the θ-sweep tab exactly. Use the gain, and compare designs on the same settings.

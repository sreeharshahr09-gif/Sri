6 — ITERATIVE SOLUTION ALGORITHM
The belt deflection y(x) and the tread loads qy(x) are mutually dependent — belt deflection changes the tread deformation, which changes the loads, which change the deflection. The model resolves this with a successive-substitution iteration:
  1. Start with y(x) = 0 (rigid belt assumption)
  2. Compute tread loads qy(x) = b·min(Cy·δy, μqz) from current y
  3. Solve the beam equation: EI·y⁽⁴⁾ + kf·y = qy (Gaussian elimination with pivoting)
  4. Update: ynew = 0.5·ybeam + 0.5·yold  (under-relaxation for stability)
  5. Repeat until max|ynew−yold| < 10⁻⁷ m  (typically 5–25 iterations)
The simply-supported boundary conditions (y=0 at both ends) mean the belt ends cannot deflect. The beam endpoints exert shear reactions R₀ = EI·y‴(0) and RN = −EI·y‴(L) which are absent from the qy integral but contribute to Mz. These are added as a post-correction: ΔMz = xc·(R₀−RN).

🎯 Design Optimisation

Theoretical basis. This tab implements three deterministic optimisation methods suited to the mathematical structure of the Brush–Fiala model. Because all output metrics (Cα, αsl, Fy, Mz, trail) are smooth and monotonic functions of the design variables — there are no local optima — gradient-free stochastic methods such as genetic algorithms are unnecessary and would be 100–300× slower for identical accuracy.

Section 1 — Sensitivity ranking (Tornado diagram). Each design variable is perturbed by ±10% of its physical range while all others are held fixed. The resulting change in the target metric is expressed as a percentage and plotted as a horizontal bar chart. This is the standard engineering Tornado diagram used in uncertainty quantification and design prioritisation. It answers: "If I can only improve one thing, which parameter gives the most leverage?"

Section 2 — Inverse solve. Uses bisection (interval halving) to find the exact value of one design variable that achieves a specified target metric value. Bisection is guaranteed to converge in ≤ log₂((hi−lo)/tol) ≈ 50 iterations for any continuous monotonic function. It answers: "What contact length L do I need to achieve Cα = 28,000 N/rad?"

Section 3 — Trade-off explorer. Sweeps one design variable across its range while simultaneously computing two output metrics. The result is plotted as a parametric curve (metric₁ vs metric₂), with the current design highlighted. This reveals whether a trade-off is steep or flat, and where your design sits on it. It answers: "If I increase Cy to gain cornering stiffness, how much does the sliding onset angle αsl decrease?"
Section 1 — Sensitivity Ranking (Tornado Diagram)

Which design parameter has the most influence?

Each bar shows the percentage change in the target metric when that parameter is perturbed by ±10% of its physical range. All 19 design variables are evaluated simultaneously. Blue = increasing the parameter increases the metric; red = increasing it decreases it. Parameters with bars near zero have negligible influence on the chosen metric.
Section 2 — Inverse Solve (Bisection)

What value achieves my target performance?

Bisection halves the search interval at each iteration. The algorithm is guaranteed to converge to machine precision in ≤60 iterations provided the target lies within the specified range. If it fails, widen the Min/Max range or check that the target is physically achievable.
Section 3 — Trade-off Explorer

How does changing one parameter affect two outputs?

The curve is colour-coded from blue (low parameter value) to red (high). The red cross marks your current design. A steep curve means the trade-off is severe — improving one metric strongly hurts the other. A flat curve means both can be improved together by changing this variable.

🔩 Duty-Cycle Wear & Durability Estimation

Theoretical basis. Tread wear is governed by the Archard wear law: the volume of material removed is proportional to the frictional energy dissipated at the contact interface. The current Brush–Fiala model already computes Cr,lat — the lateral rolling resistance coefficient — which equals the sliding energy dissipated per unit normal force per unit distance [J/(N·m)]. The wear power per unit distance is therefore: Ẇ = Cr,lat × Fz [J/m].

Duty cycle integration. A duty cycle describes the statistical distribution of operating conditions a tyre experiences over its life. Each condition i is characterised by (Fz_i, α_i, κ_i, % time_i, speed_i). The model evaluates Cr,lat at each condition, weights by distance fraction, and sums to give the duty-cycle-averaged wear energy rate [J/km]. This is the standard approach used in tyre endurance prediction and vehicle homologation.

Absolute life estimate. If the tread wear coefficient k [mm³/J] is known from compound characterisation testing, the estimated tyre life is: Life [km] = Tread volume [mm³] / (k [mm³/J] × Wear rate [J/km]). Typical passenger car compound k values range from 0.002–0.02 mm³/J. Without k, the tool gives relative wear severity — sufficient for comparing designs or operating conditions.

Limitations. This model is isothermal and does not capture thermal softening of the rubber compound (significant above ~150 km/h or sustained high-slip cornering). Lateral wear distribution across the tread width is not resolved. Belt cord and carcass structural fatigue require separate fracture mechanics modelling. The ±30–50% life estimate accuracy stated reflects these omissions and the inherent scatter in compound wear coefficients.
